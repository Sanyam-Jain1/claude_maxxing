Research note · geometric probability on polyhedral surfaces

How often is the shortest path across a cube not the obvious one?

Pick two points at random on the surface of a cube and join them by the shortest path that stays on the surface. This note gives an exact answer when the points lie on adjacent faces, and an exact description plus a 30-digit value when they lie on opposite faces. The opposite-face case was estimated on a grid in a 2020 paper, which remarked that exact computation seemed very difficult.

The question

Let S and T be independent, uniformly distributed points on the surface of a unit cube, and let d(S, T) be the length of the shortest path between them that stays on the surface. Three precise questions:

  1. If S and T are on adjacent faces, what is the probability that the shortest path does not cross their common edge and instead passes through a third face?
  2. If they are on opposite faces, what is the probability that the shortest path passes through two of the side faces (four faces in total) instead of one?
  3. What is the mean of d(S, T)?

How this question was chosen

I looked for a question where a specific published statement marks the edge of what is known, and where a claim could be tested by calculation today. Candidates that were examined and set aside:

  • Sandpile groups of chess-piece graphs. The rook's graph is solved (Ducey, Gerhard and Watson). I found no treatment of the king's or bishop's graph, but these graphs have little symmetry and I had no reason to expect a clean structure, so a search there risked ending in a table of numbers with no claim.
  • Exact mean geodesic distance on a polyhedron. Searches turned up exact results for straight-line distances inside and between faces of a cube, but none for distance measured along the surface. This led to the next item.
  • Shortest paths between opposite faces of a cube. Goldstone, Roca and Suzzi Valli (2020) show that such a path crosses three or four faces, estimate the four-face probability on a grid for each source point, and write that "exact computations seem very difficult." They also list features of their probability plot "whose explanation remains to be uncovered." That is a documented gap with a checkable answer, so I pursued it.

Setup

Unfold the faces a path crosses into the plane. The path is shortest when it becomes a straight segment, so d(S, T) is the minimum, over sequences of faces, of the straight-line distance between S and the unfolded image of T. Two facts make everything below work:

  • proved here The difference of two such squared distances has no S² or T² terms, because unfoldings are rigid motions. It is affine in S for fixed T and affine in T for fixed S. So an inequality between two unfoldings that holds at all 16 pairs of face corners holds everywhere.
  • proved here Using that fact, an exact integer check over all face sequences that visit each face at most once shows that only 3 unfoldings can ever win for adjacent faces (out of 26) and only 12 for opposite faces (out of 28). Every other sequence is beaten everywhere by a single one of these.

One step is taken from the literature: the smallest unfolded distance is always realised by a genuine path (Goldstone et al., Appendix A). I also checked it numerically on 8,000 random pairs with a brute-force search that tests whether each segment stays inside its unfolding.

Adjacent faces: the 3π/4 rule and ln 2 − 1/2

Let p, q be the distances from S to the shared edge and to the third face, and r, w the same for T. Then

ddirect² = (p + r)² + (q − w)²,    dthird² = (p + w)² + (q + r)²

and the difference factors: ddirect² − dthird² = 2[r(p − q) − w(p + q)]. So the route through the third face is shorter exactly when

w / r < (p − q) / (p + q)

Geometrically: the surface angle at a cube vertex is 3π/2, and the shortest path passes V on the side where the angle between VS and VT is less than 3π/4. The condition depends only on the two ratios σ = q/p and τ = w/r. For a uniform point in a unit square, each ratio has density 1/2 on [0, 1]. Therefore, for one end of the edge,

P = ∫01 ½ · ½ · (1 − σ)/(1 + σ) dσ = (2 ln 2 − 1)/4

The two ends of the shared edge give disjoint events (shown by a short inequality: both conditions at once would need the two bounds on T to sum to more than 1, and they never do). Doubling gives the result.

Opposite faces: six inequalities

Fix a vertical edge of the cube and call the two side faces that meet there A and B. Let x, y be the distances from S (on the bottom) to faces A and B, and let v, u be the distances from T (on the top) to faces A and B. The path bottom → A → B → top has squared length (1 + x + u)² + (y + v)². It is the shortest of all twelve candidates exactly when

InequalityIt beats the path through
(i)u(1 + x + y) < v(1 + x − y)A only
(ii)x(1 + u + v) < y(1 + u − v)B only
(iii)x(1 + u) < (1 − v)(1 + y)B, then the face opposite A
(iv)u(1 + x) < (1 − y)(1 + v)the face opposite B, then A
(v)4x + u(1 + x + y) + v(3 − x + y) < 4the face opposite A only
(vi)4u + x(1 + u + v) + y(3 − u + v) < 4the face opposite B only

By symmetry there are eight such regions of equal volume, so the four-face probability is 8 times the volume of this one region in the unit 4-cube.

computed here Sufficiency of these six was checked two ways. On a 150 × 150 grid of rational source points, exact arithmetic confirmed at every vertex of every polygon cut out by (i)–(vi) that none of the other eleven unfoldings is shorter; because the comparison is affine in T, the vertex check covers each whole polygon. Separately, Monte Carlo sampling against the brute-force minimum gave matching results. This is not a symbolic proof for every source point.

Two wrong turns on the way

Inequalities (i) and (ii) alone looked sufficient at first. They are the two "corner moves" and reduce to ratio conditions like the adjacent-face rule. That region integrates to 0.15980, but brute-force sampling gave 0.15588. Adding (iii) and (iv) still left a discrepancy of about 10−4, traced to (v) and (vi). The two-inequality value survives as a rigorous upper bound:

P4 < 2(2IA + IB) = 0.1598041421674…,    IA = ½ − 2 ln 2 + π√3/6

IB = 0.0386914290892751795… is a one-dimensional integral of logarithms over (1 + s²)³; I did not find a closed form for it.

The value

For a fixed source point the target region is a convex polygon whose area is a rational function of (x, y). There are seven polygon types. Integrating the exact area formulas over the regions where each type applies, with region boundaries located by root-finding, gives

Result 2. computed here P4 = 0.155880815524256750509896159877…

The polygon type changes across curves in the (x, y) plane. One is the circle x² + (y + 1)² = 2. Three are cubics, for example

x³ − x²y + xy² − y³ − 2x² − 2xy − 3x + y = 0

heuristic A computer-algebra check found no singular points on any of these cubics, in the affine plane or at infinity, so each has genus 1. Integrating rational functions over a region bounded by a genus-1 curve usually produces elliptic integrals. This is a reason to doubt that P4 has an expression in logarithms, arctangents and dilogarithms. It is not a proof: a different order of integration could avoid these curves.

open Integer-relation searches (PSLQ at 40 digits, coefficients up to 2000) against four sets of constants built from π, √2, √3, √7, logarithms of 2, 3 and 7, Catalan's constant and several dilogarithm values returned only relations at the size expected by chance.

The source-point map, and the corner behaviour

Goldstone et al. plot, for each source point, the fraction of target points reached by a four-face path, and describe "apparent singularities at the corners" as unexplained. The exact polygons explain them. As S approaches a corner along a ray making angle θ with the nearer edge, the fraction tends to a limit that depends on the direction:

proved here with κ = tan(π/4 − θ):   limit = κ² / (2(1 − κ)) for κ ≤ ½, and (1 − κ) − (1 − κ)³/(2κ²) + (2κ − 1)²/(2κ²) for κ ≥ ½.

The limit runs from 0 along the diagonal to 1/2 along the edge. The function is bounded but has no single value at the corner, which is what a contour plot draws as a pinch. The low values along the diagonals have the same origin: on the diagonal through a corner, that corner's region has zero area.

Against, or weaker than it looks.

  • The Monte Carlo estimate for the adjacent case sits 1.8 standard errors above ln 2 − 1/2. That is within ordinary sampling variation, and the derivation is short enough to check by hand, but it is the weakest agreement in the table.
  • Digits of P4 beyond the ninth are supported only by repeated runs of one method at different working precisions (20, 32 and 42 digits). The independent method confirms about nine digits.
  • The opposite-face mean distance has no high-precision derivation here, only the clipping method (convergent to about 10 digits) and Monte Carlo.
  • Result 1 is elementary. A result this short may exist as folklore or as an exercise that my searches did not reach.

What might be new

  • The exact value ln 2 − 1/2 for adjacent faces.
  • The six-inequality description of the four-face region, including the finding that the two corner-move conditions alone overcount by 2.5%.
  • The numerical value of the four-face probability, and the upper bound 0.15980.
  • The direction-dependent corner limit, which accounts for the unexplained corner feature in the 2020 plot.
  • The mean surface distance 1.0629753 for the unit cube and its three components.
  • The observation that the natural region boundaries are smooth cubics.

What remains unverified

  • Novelty. The search covered arXiv listings, general web search and the two most relevant papers. I did not read the journal version of the 2020 paper, the full text of Fontenot et al., or search MathOverflow, Math Stack Exchange or the problem sections of journals systematically.
  • Sufficiency of the six inequalities for every source point, as opposed to the 22,500 rational points tested exactly.
  • Whether P4 has a closed form. The genus argument is suggestive only.
  • Closed forms for IB and for the adjacent-face mean distance.
  • The realisability lemma is cited from Goldstone et al. and checked numerically, not re-proved.

The simplest test

Twelve lines of Python sample both probabilities without using any of the reasoning above. With 10⁷ samples the standard error is about 0.00012; to distinguish the claimed P4 from the two-inequality value 0.15980 takes about 10⁵ samples.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0); N = 10**7
a, b, c, d = rng.random((4, N))
# adjacent: S=(a,b) on the bottom, T on the face x=1 at position c, height d
direct = (1+d-a)**2 + (c-b)**2
around = np.minimum((2-c-a)**2 + (1+d-b)**2, (1+c-a)**2 + (d+b)**2)
print((around < direct).mean(), np.log(2) - 0.5)
# opposite: S=(a,b) on the bottom, T=(c,d) on the top
three = np.minimum.reduce([(b-d)**2+(3-a-c)**2, (b-d)**2+(1+a+c)**2,
                           (a-c)**2+(3-b-d)**2, (a-c)**2+(1+b+d)**2])
four = np.minimum.reduce([(3-d-a)**2+(2-c-b)**2, (3-c-b)**2+(2-d-a)**2,
    (2-d+a)**2+(1+c-b)**2, (2+c-b)**2+(1-d+a)**2, (2+d-a)**2+(1-c+b)**2,
    (2-c+b)**2+(1+d-a)**2, (1+d+a)**2+(c+b)**2, (1+c+b)**2+(d+a)**2])
print((four < three).mean(), 0.1558808155)

A sharper test of Result 1 needs no computer: verify the factorisation of ddirect² − dthird² and evaluate one integral.
